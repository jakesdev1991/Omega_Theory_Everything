\section*{Layman's Summary}
The theory of computation studies what can be computed and what cannot. Cantor's diagonal argument and the halting problem are its classic results. This volume records Cantor's argument and a toy model of halting. It does not prove general results about physical computation or about P versus NP.

\section{Cantor's Diagonal Argument (Formal Result)}
There is no surjection from $\mathbb{N}$ onto the set of infinite binary sequences. This is a standard, fully general proof.

\section{Toy Computability Semantics (Formal Result within the Model)}
The file defines a simple step-function model of computation over natural-number configurations. In this model, bounded halting is decidable, and a diagonal machine shows that no enumeration of toy machines captures all halting behavior. This is not a Turing-machine model, and it is not equivalent to one.

\section{Church--Turing--Deutsch Principle (Model Assumption, Not Formalized)}
The earlier claim that every physical system can be perfectly simulated by a universal quantum computer is a principle from the literature, not a result of this volume. It is not formalized here, and it is withdrawn as a claim of this work.

\section{Gödel and Undecidability (Not Formalized)}
The earlier claim that Gödel's incompleteness and undecidability follow from geometric paradoxes of the universe has no formal counterpart. The toy halting diagonal is not Gödel's theorem. The claim is withdrawn.

\section{P versus NP (No Formal Counterpart)}
The earlier claim that P is not equal to NP, as a physical wall created by informational stiffness, has no formal counterpart. The P versus NP problem is open in the standard literature, and this work does not resolve it. The claim is withdrawn and is listed as an open item.

\section{Scope and Open Items}
